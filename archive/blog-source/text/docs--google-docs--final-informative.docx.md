This is Erin Cross, a 6-year-old living in the United Kingdom. She was first diagnosed with a type of leukemia in 2012 and has been receiving treatment for three years. Not a single treatment was working, Erin's parents were told that their only hope for beating this leukemia was to receive a new type of treatment, gene therapy. The doctors of Royal Manchester Children's Hospital in the United Kingdom said that this therapy was only being done in the United States and that they only had 8 weeks until they would lose her. Her family decided it was worth a shot and flew to Seattle. In August of 2016, Erin received gene therapy at Seattle Children's Hospital. She was one of the first in the world to trial this new gene-therapy treatment. As of this past December 26th,  her family was told by the doctors that the blood tests revealed the cancer had completely disappeared. This is only one cancer success of gene-therapy. However, there are other diseases that are also finding hope with this breakthrough treatment. Gene therapy is a process where doctors use a corrective gene, created in a laboratory, and insert it into a patient's diseased cell. In order to understand this promising treatment and its benefits, we will first diagnose what gene therapy is. We will then examine what it can cure, and finally we will check up on what the future holds.

In order to have a basic understanding of how gene therapy works, we need to diagnose some charts that will explain the process. According to St. Jude's Research Hospital this picture shows what gene therapy is. If a cell has DNA that doesn’t work it is known as a defective gene. A corrective gene is a gene with healthy DNA. In order to fix the functions that the gene doesn't have, a corrective gene is introduced into the cell. When the corrective gene is introduced into the cell, it overrides of the defective gene. If this goes smoothly, cell function is restored. Now that we understand the bigger picture we need to actually go more in depth on the overall process. First in gene therapy, a doctor draws blood from a patient. The doctor then takes the blood into a laboratory to see what is wrong with the cell and then determines what type of gene is needed to replace the defective gene. After they do this, 4 they introduce a vector into the process. a gene that is inserted directly into a cell usually does not function. Therefore, a carrier called a vector is introduced. A vector is a virus that has been modified so it is able to deliver a gene to a cell because it can merge into the cell without causing harm. 5 Once a vector is introduced, the gene is inserted inside of it. 6 After this, they release the vector into the blood in hopes of it merging into a cell. As we can see, the vector merges into the cell and releases the gene into the nucleus. The cell then reproduces. 7When the cell reproduces enough times, the blood gets injected back into the body and then continues to replace the defective cells.

Now that we understand the process of creating a healthy gene, we can relate the good news for people suffering from some common fatal diseases. Before Erin received treatment, two professors at the University of Texas were treating five leukemia patients with gene therapy. Of those 5 patients, 4 of them remained in complete remission five months later. This caught the University of Pennsylvania's eye in 2015 and they started their own trial involving 59 leukemia patients. According to Laurence Cooper MD, These patients were facing a certain death when they received the gene therapy. 26 of the 59 patients, including 19 children, are now cancer-free.

Another serious health concern is a severe combined immunodeficiency or SCID. This is David Vetter who was born in 1971 and had to live in this bubble his entire life. He was a sufferer of SCID, a disease that leaves males with little to no immune protection. Due to this, children like David would have to take major precautions so they wouldn't die. Since there wasn't a cure, little David died in 1984. However,  St. Jude Children's Research Hospital has had recent results from April of 2016 that gave SCID patients a hope so they no longer have to live with these conditions. Two patients were first given the therapy and they showed high levels of immune cells and corrected cells. After the success in this trial, they had three other patients and they too recovered from this disease.

At approximately the same time as the St. Jude trials, a team at Oxford University was wrapping up a gene therapy trial dealing with a rare case disorder involving hereditary blindness. Until now, there has been no treatment and the young men with this disorder gradually become blind. The University of Oxford examined the vision of six patients and decided to do the gene therapy on them. Dr. Stephen Caddick said that this was the first time they've seen a permanent change in vision with gene therapy. All of the patients saw a great improvement. 9 In looking at these pictures of a patient's brain before and 3 months after gene therapy, The brain's responses are shown in yellow and orange and are located in the area of the brain responsible for visual function.

For the diseases of leukemia, immunodeficiency, and hereditary blindness, gene therapy gives hope for the future: But more work needs to be done in order for this therapy to expand to other diseases. As of 2016, Dr. Bennett of University of Pennsylvania, says that the development of new vectors is one of the areas of gene therapy that’s moving quickly. In the next 5 years, doctors and researchers will be improving vectors in a very basic yet important way....their size. As of now, one of the biggest issues with vectors is the size of gene that can fit inside of them. According to Dr. Bennett "it would be great if there were further developments that allowed these vectors to package larger amounts of cargo. For an example, if doctors are able to double the size of what could be packaged inside of a vector, it would be wonderful."  After hearing Dr. Bennett’s input, researchers realize that there are so many types of genes and that some of them are just simply too large to fit into the current vectors developed. Once the improvements with the vectors are made, Dr. MacLaren, professor of ophthalmology at the University of Oxford believes that in less than five years as of 2016, the vectors we are currently seeing trials of now, will be sold just like any other drug and it will cause a dramatic change in the pharmaceutical business. The future appearance of vector treatments will require an entirely new business model for the pharmaceutical industry. Therefore, the cost of this therapy is currently between 100 thousand and 1 million dollars and will still be in the future. The healthcare system and government are currently trying to find a business plan that would work to fund gene therapy. Professor Elizabeth Rakoczy of the University of Western Australia remarks that "even if the solution on funding right now is uncertain, it is certain that gene therapy will be used for treating a tremendous number of different diseases."
