Tales from the Bed by Jennifer Estes

I remember March 17, 1997, being a very windy day in New York City. As I attempted to catch a cab, I saw my whole life coming toward me: I saw a husband and children, as true possibilities. There I was between them, walking confidently. I looked pretty good. The wind really picked up. I felt instant relief in my office, a luxury midtown high-rise with great air-conditioning and a view of Central Park – salary so not commensurate. But the twitches in my legs that I had seen occasionally over the last few months were going wild. My thigh muscles moved like snakes. It was bizarre – there were twitches in my arms, too, but I didn’t want to look down at them. I wanted to look anywhere else. I wanted to look out of my window at the thousandths of people walking, running, pushing, strolling, and kissing through Central Park. But the muscles of my legs and arms were rolling like the sea. As usual, my heart knew what my head would learn: something was seriously wrong with me.

INTRO

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis is a disease that destroys the nervous system and gradually causes physical disability. More commonly known to us as ALS, or Lou Gherig’s Disease, it affects approximately 20,000 people in the United States alone. Jenifer Estess was a young businesswoman with aspirations of marriage and a family of her own. However, those dreams quickly took a detour when she was diagnosed with ALS at the age of 35. Rather than spending her last days grieving, Jenifer helped raise $17 million for her foundation, project ALS, made popular recently by the ice bucket challenge. In her final moments ,Jennifer forces us to ask ourselves, when death stares us in the face, how will we be remembered? Tales from the Bed: A Memoir, by Jenifer Estess.

To look at me you couldn’t tell anything was wrong.  ALS was a real creep that way – it waited til I was alone so it could whisper in my ear.  It whispered to me while I was shaving my legs.  I shaved my left leg – truth be told, the muscle atrophy from ALS had my legs looking very thin..  But when it came time for me to put my right leg up onto the marble shelf, I couldn’t – quite – do it.  I tried kicking it up again, but no go.  Okay . . . so I wouldn’t shave my right leg.  European women didn’t shave either leg and everyone still loved them.  ALS would take away something relatively small, like shaving my right leg, knowing that I would immediately rationalize the loss.  Nature designed human beings to rationalize loss. Without it, we’d all go off the deep end. Although I couldn’t shave my right leg, I figured I still looked healthier than ever – I didn’t look sick.

But, little by little, my inalienable rights to life, liberty and the pursuit of errands were being violated by ALS.  “All right let’s do it.  To the dry cleaners,” I ordered my body, gripping my cane.  My body ignored me.  I stood motionless for minutes.  Come on Jen. Just put one foot in front of the other.

As I soon learned, ALS was less and less forgiving. The Yankee baseball legend Lou Gherig died from it, and so did every single one of the hundreds of thousands of other people who got it. No one survived. There was no medicine for ALS. I was thirty-five years old and I was dying.

My two sisters and I had always taken care of one another, and it occurred to us that we kids could bring our business skills to bear on science.  We could raise money, we could lock to smartest scientists in a room and put a gun to their heads until they came up with a cure.  Instead we founded Project ALS.  It was absolutely essential that scientists and doctors and government officials worked together to make sick people better.

I focused like a laser on Project ALS.  I learned a million ways to say the same thing, “the state of ALS was unacceptable, Project ALS was going to change it.”  I told the Health and Human Services Committees  that ALS was a national disaster that needed the government’s attention immediately. I reminded the government subcommittee that people blindsided by life-threatening illnesses and injuries had the same American Dream as everyone else.  Each day I speak from inside my body, which has now become a prison.

My breathing had become even more labored.  My diaphragm and my chest muscles had grown weak.  A bi-pap was prescribed.  It was a truly unattractive ventilator that would force air into my lungs and suck it out through my nose – in an unending cycle of assisted breathing.  Basically, I was now wearing a vacuum cleaner to stay alive.

When do you wave the white flag? I had to be realistic. I knew that I would die soon.

I don’t know what’s next for me, if there is a next. If there is, I’d be grateful for a skating rink of some kind. The ice was always so beautiful in my life. My sisters and I would hold on to one another, skating in a circle until the night came, falling on to our backs on the ice. I’d look up at the stars and take a deep breath. Oh my, the stars. I wanted to touch them and knew that someday, in some way, I would. So here I am. I’d be grateful for a skating rink. I hope there’s a snack bar. Meantime, I can say about death what I knew about life: It’s not about letting go, but reaching.
