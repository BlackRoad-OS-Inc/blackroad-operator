SERIES 7 SUITABILITY EXERCISE
AN EXERCISE DESIGNED TO IMPROVE YOUR SUITABILITY AND 
RECOMMENDATIONS QUESTION ANSWERING SKILLS
The following exercise has been designed to assist you with answering scenario-based questions 
which target suitability and making recommendations. This is a skill dependent on your 
ability to associate different product characteristics, including the level of risk associated with 
the product, and aligning those associations with different client profiles and life scenarios. 
As you will see, there is no “one size fits all” grid or matrix, but rather utilizing only the 
information given and then aligning with the best answer choice of those given. This exercise 
will sharpen those abilities.
Included in this package is a piece posted for our students on the Exam Tips & Content 
Updates link. This primer addresses how best to approach suitability and recommendations 
questions. The piece is titled, Suitability and Recommendation questions… Series 7 style. 
If you haven’t already read this piece, please do so before proceeding.
(See Appendix A.)
This exercise requires three steps:
1.
Become comfortable with the suitability association roadmaps presented in this exercise.
These roadmaps will begin to hone your associations for specific products and their vari­
ous characteristics and risks, as well as present nonproduct suitability factors that will
need to be considered in scenario questions.
––
Suitability Association Roadmap – Product Characteristics
––
Suitability Association Roadmap – Nonproduct Characteristics
2.
Review the scenarios and choose the product or investment strategy that most aligns with
the customer profile presented (investment objective and/or risk tolerance). In the begin­
ning you will be presented with two choices, but as you progress through the exercise,
choices will be broadened to three in some scenarios. As you work through them, your
sensibilities to the relevant factors presented should heighten, better enabling you to
choose correctly.
3.
Review the rationales for each scenario to identify the dominant factors presented in the
scenarios that should have guided you to the best choice.

We all know from our Series 7 studies that suitability and recommendation issues crop up on 
FINRA’s punch list regarding your preparation to become licensed, registered representatives. 
Translating content into the skills required to choose the correct answer on test day is the issue 
at hand.
Here are insights into what to look for when you get a question on suitability.
There is no chart (simple or otherwise) to point a student to the correct answer in these 
types of questions because the issue is generally not that clear cut. In suitability and 
recommendation questions many factors must be considered, for instance:

And of course there could be others, as the previous list is not all-inclusive.
It is important to understand that not every question will address all of those factors, but 
every question must supply information on one or two of them. Only the factors stated in the 
question should ever be considered. In other words, don’t assume anything beyond those that 
have been supplied. The assumption of factors to be considered that were never mentioned in 
the question is a common pitfall that students are apt to drop into.
However, there are “clues” within the questions to look out for, and often they have to do 
more with eliminating incorrect answer choices which generally makes the correct one a bit 
easier to target.
Here are just a few general examples:


If you were told that a customer is in a marginal tax bracket or is employed at a lower
income level:
Eliminate any tax-favored investments such as municipal bonds, as the tax benefits of 
those securities wouldn’t help that customer to any worthwhile extent. DPPs where tax 
benefits are a major motivator would also likely be unsuitable.


If a customer has a very short-term investment time horizon (needs the money soon or
within the next year or so):
Eliminate most mutual fund investments as they are not intended to be short-term 
investment vehicles. Most share classes of mutual funds are for long-term investing, not 
short-term trading, or meeting current financial goals. If an investor is more likely to 
trade in and out of mutual funds frequently for any reason, target C class shares as being 
more suitable.
