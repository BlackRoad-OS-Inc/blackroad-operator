Eve-olution 	 F Drama

By Hilary Illick and Jennifer Krier

I have a baby girl, Lucy, and I just landed the most prestigious job in my field – a tenure track position at Cornell University.  My dream job:  Professor of Anthropology at an Ivy League school.  But, it turns out my husband, Leo, won’t be relocating to Ithaca.  He already has his dream job in Manhattan.  It’s just going to be me and Lucy.  Leo will fly out every weekend and Lucy can go to daycare.

It won’t be that bad.  We’re both getting to pursue our dreams.  I call my mom and she says,  “Allison, when are you going to spend time with Lucy?  And what about your marriage?”  “Mom, I have to take this job.  It would be insane not to.  I want this.”

According to CBS News, the number of mothers who have decided to not return to work so they can stay at home to raise their children has increased by 15% since 2001. It seems that more and more families are finding it hard to balance both work and our society’s expectations of motherhood. Authors Illick and Krier examine this issue as Allison, a new mother and college professor, struggles to find this elusive balance.

Eve-olution by Hilary Illick and Jennifer Krier.

I bring three-year-old Lucy with me to the airport and when Leo comes off the plane, I say, “It’s been a week from hell Leo.  I have a huge pile of papers to grade tonight.  When we get home I need you to take Lucy and put her to bed so that I can get right to work.”  It wasn’t exactly the greeting he expected, and it’s not the one that I intended either.  But I’ve been a single working mom and now that he’s here, I need his help.

We have our second baby, Ruby, and our weekends turn into the string of fights about who gets time to nap, who gets time out to do their work, and who’s “on duty” with the kids.

Leo’s alarm is set for 4am so he can catch the first flight back to the city and I say, “Leo, our weekend is almost over and we haven’t yet had a single real conversation.”

It’s time to get up.  Leo’s gone.  I make breakfast for Lucy and Ruby but I can’t eat, I have to get ready for work.  But I feel sick.  I believe I leave my girls every day – Ruby’s only six months old.  I put on my makeup, I get my briefcase, and I start to feel better.  I’m Professor Near.  My office is in the building with the tower.  I own this campus.  Cornell University Press wants to publish my dissertation.  I make lists every day.  On my lists, I love to put a square next to each item, so I can put a check in each box.

I have to plan everything perfectly so I can do it all.  Pack lunch for Lucy, check voice mail, go running, plan tomorrow’s lecture, rehearse lecture, grade papers, pump breast milk.

But grades were due yesterday.  My department cut me some slack my first year, but my grace period is over and I need to deliver.  My student evaluations last semester weren’t great.  Well actually, my Chair said that they were the worst he’s ever read.  The worst ever.

I want to ask for help.  But I’m afraid they’ll think I don’t know what I’m doing, and… I don’t even think about my kids.

Every day I let them down.  I say I’ll come home early, but I never do.  I never can.  And when I do let myself think about my children, I feel such utter sadness.  But I have to stop feeling so that I can stay functional through another day.  So I can do it all.

But I love my kids so much more than I planned.

My friend Beth is over with her toddler to play with me and Lucy and Ruby.  Beth is getting out all of these new cans of play-dough and cookie cutters, and play dough tools.  Beth has it totally under control.  She’s read all the parenting books – and not only that, but she’s enjoying herself.  Beth doesn’t have a title.  But she seems to know who she is.  Beth’s daughter crawls into her lap for a cuddle.  And Beth is staring into her daughter’s eyes, and smoothing back her daughter’s hair.  I can see Beth lose herself in this moment of delicious intimacy with her child.  I thought I’d be a better mother.

It’s a Sunday morning, when Lucy comes storming in, “Come on, let’s get up and play outside!” she says.  I open my eyes to see that the yard is covered with about a foot of snow.  Lucy’s eyes are sparkling.  But I’m so sleepy, so I just keep putting her off.  “Let me wake up.”  I say.  “Give me a minute, I just want to drink my coffee first.”  She begins to throw a tantrum.  How did this happen?  It’s a beautiful Sunday morning, and I’m getting screamed at.   Lucy’s in a heap on the floor beside me, whimpering.  Snowgear is strewn all across the room, and Lucy is writhing and jackknifing still simply out of control, kicking everything in sight.  And Ruby is toddling around looking a little bit scared and she goes over to Lucy and she smacks her!  And Lucy screams – “Mo-om!”  But if Lucy screams like that one more time—

“Lucy!  Stop it!  Stop it right now!”

I slap her.

I hit my child.  There’s a look of complete betrayal in her eyes.

All I want to do is take this poor confused child into my arms.  Shhhhh. It’s okay.  Shhhh.  It’s okay.   Shhhh….. Mommy’s so sorry…… I carry Lucy to her room and settle her in bed.  I go to the kitchen to make Lucy something special but all we have is oatmeal.  I thought I’d be a better mother.  I thought I’d have waffles and bacon and strawberries on hand.  I thought I’d have more patience.  Every day  ….. I let them down.

The sense of self I had before I become a mother hasn’t survived.  But …..something else is being born.  When people ask me things like, “What happened to the anthropologist?”  I’ll say, “I don’t know.”  Because what’s being born is the woman who is okay with not knowing.
